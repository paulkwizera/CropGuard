Our story

Behind every harvest is a decision made without enough information.

Rwanda's farmers decide when to plant, which seed to use and when to spray, often with little advice and no way to see a disease early. We studied the national agricultural household survey (AHS2024) to see what is really happening on the farm. This is what the numbers say, and why we built CropGuard.

Survey sample: farming households (AHS2024)

1What Rwanda grows

Maize is on almost every farm.

Maize is the most widely grown crop in the survey, ahead of bananas, sweet potato and beans. Cassava is also widespread: almost six in ten farming households grow it.

A tool that spots maize and cassava diseases reaches the crops most families already depend on.

2The harvest is under pressure

More than half of crop plots had a smaller harvest than the season before.

Farmers compared this season's production with the one before. For maize, cassava and all crops together, "smaller" is more common than "bigger". The survey does not say why, but disease, pests and weather are risks every farmer faces.

Catching a disease early is one of the few things a farmer can still do while the crop is growing.

3Rain decides

Almost every plot depends on the rain.

Only a small share of plots is irrigated, and many farmers say there is no water to irrigate with. When the rain is late or heavy there is no backup, so knowing the weather a day ahead helps decide when to plant, weed, spray or harvest.

CropGuard's weather plan turns the next 24 hours into simple advice on irrigating and spraying.

4Seeds

Seed quality decides the harvest before the first rain.

Most maize plots now use improved seed. For cassava almost everyone still plants traditional cuttings, and improved varieties are very rare. Cassava is grown from cuttings, so a disease in the planting material is passed on to the next crop.

Healthy planting material and early disease detection protect both this season's harvest and the next one.

5Advice

Most farmers get little advice, and almost none by phone.

One in three households received no advisory service at all. Only a small share were trained in pest management or given weather information, and crop insurance is rare.

CropGuard puts a first opinion and weather advice in the farmer's pocket, in Kinyarwanda.

6Phones

Phones are everywhere. Smartphones are not, yet.

Most households have someone with a phone, but fewer than half of people own one and only about one in five owners has a smartphone. So CropGuard is built to be light: photos are shrunk before they are sent and pages work on a basic data plan. Extension officers and cooperatives already visit farmers, and they can use CropGuard to help people without a smartphone.

This is a real limit: today CropGuard needs a phone with a camera and a web browser.

Where we want to go next

  • More crops. After maize and cassava, the survey points to cooking banana, sweet potato and beans, each grown by about six in ten households.
  • Reach farmers without smartphones through extension officers and cooperatives.
  • Test the detectors with farmers in the field, and keep improving them with real photos.

About these numbers

  • Source: the AHS2024 agricultural household survey microdata kept in the project's data folder.
  • Figures are counts and shares of the surveyed households and crop plots. They are not weighted to the whole country, so read them as what the survey sample shows.
  • "Smaller" or "bigger" harvest is what farmers reported compared with the previous season. The survey does not record the cause.
  • Harvest per hectare uses plots with a recorded harvest, area and plausible values, and shows the middle value so a few extreme records do not distort it.
  • Phone figures come from household members who were asked about phones; internet use is within the previous three months.
  • To rebuild the numbers: python data/build_story_data.py
